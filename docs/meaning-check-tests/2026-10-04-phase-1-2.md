# Meaning check test — 2026-10-04, Phase 1.2 (numbers, "not" words, inclusive pronouns)

**What this is.** Phase 1.2 of the meaning check: the word-level checks that run after the similarity score. It (1) reads numbers the way people write them, (2) understands more ways English says "not", and (3) treats pronouns inclusively: singular *they* and neopronouns count, a gender appearing that you didn't write is flagged, and in languages that can't carry gender through the round trip the app says so instead of implying it's fine.

**Summary:** on the held-out translations of runs 2 and 3 (11 languages), false alarms on good translations fall from **4.6% to 3.5%** and **4.8% to 4.1%**, with errors caught unchanged (**77% → 77%**, **78% → 79%**). No language got worse. As predicted before starting, word rules mostly cut false alarms; catching more errors needs Phase 3.

## What changed, and why each rule works

### 1. Numbers people write differently

The number check compares the numbers in your text with those in the ↩ line. A changed number is a real error (run 1: the similarity model barely notices "15" → "52"), but a correct translation can state the same number differently. Each rule below is one of those ways, found in runs 2–3's false alarms:

| Your text | ↩ line | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| finishing on the **eighteenth** | finishing on the **18th** | ordinal words count as numbers, from "fourth" on ("first" to "third" usually aren't counts: "for the first time") |
| between **340 million** and **500 million** | between **340** and **500 million** | a scale said once for two numbers: amounts that differ by exactly ×1,000 / ×1,000,000 / ×1,000,000,000 match |
| **90°F** heat | **32°C** heat | unit conversions match within 6% (°F/°C within 1.5°): temperature, distance, speed, weight |
| 3,000 miles from Miami | 3,000 miles **(4,800 km)** from Miami | a number that's only a conversion of another amount says nothing new |
| speakers of **both** languages | speakers of the **two** languages | "both", "a pair", "a couple", "twice" stand for 2; "a decade" for 10 |
| **four dozen** bases | **48** bases | dozens multiply |
| **a couple of thousand** staff | **several thousand** staff | vague amounts aren't compared at all |
| 2.2 million **km2** | 2.2 million **km 2** | the "2" of km² isn't a count |

Unit-tested both ways (`numbersMatch`): the forms above match, and real changes still don't (1977 ≠ 1997, 90°F ≠ 50°C, 220 km ≠ 520 km, 15 ≠ 52).

### 2. More ways to say "not"

The negation check counts negations on each side; if one side has an odd number and the other even, a "not" appeared or disappeared ("I can come" ↩ "I cannot come"). Two negations cancel ("not unrelated" = related), which the count handles for free. The risk is a word that negates on one side only, which tips the count, so each addition was checked row by row:

| Added | Why |
|---|---|
| un-words: unharmed, unwise, unrelated, unbreakable, unusable… | "not hurt" ↩ "unharmed" was flagged |
| a list of in-/im-/ir-/il- words: immoral, indestructible, irrelevant… | "unethical" ↩ "immoral". A list, not a rule: "important", "include", "image" also start with im-/in- |
| "without X" -less words: treeless, homeless, useless, harmless… | "treeless" ↩ "without trees" |
| doubt, absence | "unsure" ↩ "doubt" |
| phrases that look negative but aren't: "not long ago" (recently), "not far from" (near), "no matter", "no doubt", "don't worry" (rest assured), "No. 9" (number 9) | each was a false alarm |

**Tried and taken out** (they made things worse on the data): *rare* (as in "rarely"): "exotic animals" ↩ "rare animals" became a false alarm. **-ly adverbs** ("implausibly fast", "unusually"): they say *how*, not *whether*, and tipped 14 rows. **-less words meaning "very many"** (countless, boundless, endless).

### 3. Inclusive pronouns

Before, the pronoun check knew only he and she. Now:

| Your text | ↩ line | Shown |
|---|---|---|
| **They** said **they** would come. | **He** said **he** would come. | "a gendered pronoun appeared where you wrote 'they'" (capped at 60) |
| Alex finished **xyr** homework before **xe** left. | … **her** homework before **she** left. | same (misgendering) |
| Alex finished **xyr** homework… | … **their** homework… | "your pronoun 'xyr' didn't come back (most languages don't have it)": kept below 85, not "likely off", because most languages have no neopronouns, so this is the translation's limit, not an error |
| My **partner** is a doctor. | My **wife** is a doctor. | "a gender appeared: 'wife' where you wrote 'partner'" (also parent → mother, sibling → brother, child → son…) |
| **His** mother… | **Her** mother… | "a pronoun changed (he ↔ she)", as before |

Neopronouns recognized: xe/xem/xyr, ze/zir, ze/hir, ey/eir/emself, fae/faer. Singular and plural *they* look the same, so "they" → "he" is flagged either way; it's a meaning change in both cases.

**Where pronouns can't be checked.** Some languages' pronouns don't say he, she or they (Tagalog *siya*, Hindi/Urdu *vah/voh*), and Spanish drops subject pronouns and uses *su* for his/her/their. A swap or a misgendering can't come back through them, so the check can't see it. Measured on runs 2–3 (48 swapped pronouns per language):

| Language | Swapped pronouns caught |
|---|---|
| Tagalog | 12% |
| Hindi | 29% |
| Spanish | 33% |
| Urdu | 35% |
| Japanese (next lowest) | 60% |
| … Arabic (highest) | 88% |

For the four languages under 50%, when your text has a pronoun, the hover card now says **"pronouns can't be checked: Tagalog pronouns usually don't say he, she or they, so a change wouldn't come back"** instead of staying silent. This doesn't change any score; it stops the score implying more than it knows.

The hover card's wording also changed from "he/she" to "pronouns".

## What we learned

1. **Counting negations is fragile by design.** Every word added to the "not" list can tip the count on one side of a good translation. The fix isn't more words but the right words: ones that negate a fact ("unharmed", "treeless"), not ones that describe or intensify ("implausibly", "countless"). That's why each addition here was checked row by row, and three were taken back out.
2. **Some "false alarms" are the check working.** The benchmark's "good" translations are good going *out*, but Google's way back still makes real mistakes (eight for eighteen, his for their).
3. **Pronouns are a limit of the round trip itself**, not of the word lists: through Tagalog, Hindi, Urdu or Spanish, he/she/they mostly can't survive. The honest answer is to say so; the real fix is to compare your text with the translation directly (Phase 3).
4. **Word rules have done what they can for catching errors** (77–79% for three phases now). Phase 3 is where that number moves.

## Next steps

1. **Fresh held-out set** to confirm these numbers on text nobody has looked at (new FLORES sentences; about 30–40 minutes of Google requests), adding planted errors for the new pronoun cases (they → he, partner → wife).
2. **Phase 3:** compare your text with the translation directly (cross-lingual similarity with the same local model), translate back with a different engine ([#29](https://github.com/chrisqtruong/vox2/issues/29)), optional AI check ([#7](https://github.com/chrisqtruong/vox2/issues/7)). This is what can lift errors caught past 80% and catch pronoun changes in Tagalog, Hindi, Urdu and Spanish.
3. **Inclusive translation, not just checking:** instruct the AI engines to keep every person's pronouns and never assume a gender the text doesn't state (separate change).
