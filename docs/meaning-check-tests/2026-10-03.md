# Meaning check test — 2026-10-03

**Run 1, baseline.** The first measured test of Vox2's meaning check (the ↩ back-translation line and its "% match" score).

**Summary:** the check is **reliable at confirming good translations** (93% shown as "meaning kept", 4% false alarms) and **catches changed numbers every time**, but it **misses most other meaning errors**: 71% of translations that said something different still scored 85+ ("meaning kept"). Flipped negations, opposite words, swapped he/she and dropped clauses mostly score 100. The error is usually still visible in the back-translation text, so targeted checks can catch it ([#28](https://github.com/chrisqtruong/vox2/issues/28)).

## What was tested and why

Vox2 tells you how much of your meaning survived a translation: it translates the result back into your language and compares that with what you wrote. We want to lead with this feature, so we need to know how often it's right.

A useful test needs translations we *know* are good or bad. So:

- **Good translations** (should score high):
  - **Professional:** the human translation from FLORES-200, independent of Google.
  - **Google:** Google's translation of the correct English sentence.
- **Bad translations** (should *not* score high): Google's translation of the English sentence **with one planted meaning error**, so the translation is fluent but says something different. The error types (8 sentences each):

| Error | Example (original → what the bad translation says) |
|---|---|
| negation | "the ship **was** on its way…" → "the ship **was not** on its way…" |
| number | "death toll is at least **15**" → "at least **52**" |
| pronoun | "**his** wife was not hurt" → "**her** wife was not hurt" |
| opposite | "stays in **many** of these hotels" → "stays in **few** of these hotels" |
| dropped clause | "…without your knowledge, **which will land you in a great deal of trouble**." → "…without your knowledge." |

**Languages (11):** Vietnamese, Japanese, Korean, Chinese (simplified), Spanish, Arabic, Hindi, French, Tagalog, Portuguese, Urdu.

## How it was done

For each language:

1. Translate the correct English and the corrupted English into the language with Google.
2. Translate all three translations (professional, Google-correct, Google-corrupted) back into English with Google: this is the ↩ line Vox2 shows.
3. Score each back-translation against the **correct** English with Vox2's actual `matchScore()` (the same code the app runs, including the number check).

What counts:

- **Good translation shown as "meaning kept"** = score 85+ (higher is better).
- **False alarm** = a good translation scored below 65, "likely off" (lower is better).
- **Error caught** = a bad translation scored below 85, so it is *not* shown as "meaning kept" (higher is better).
- **Error flagged** = a bad translation scored below 65, "likely off" (higher is better).
- **Missed** = a bad translation scored 85+, shown as "meaning kept" (lower is better). This is the case that misleads users.

## What we noticed

1. **The similarity score barely reacts to meaning flips.** Sentence embeddings compare topic and wording. "Was chosen as the national flower" and "was not chosen as the national flower" are about the same thing, so they score 100. The same goes for opposites ("many" vs "few"), he/she, and a missing clause.
2. **The error is usually right there in the ↩ line.** In 92% of the negation errors, the back-translation contains the flipped "not". Simply comparing the count of negation words (not, no, never, can't…) between your text and the ↩ line catches 81 of the 88 negation errors (92%), and fires on only 16 of the 880 good translations (1.8%), mostly where the translator legitimately rephrased with a "no" or "don't". For he/she the swap was visible in 57% (Google sometimes "fixes" an odd pronoun on the way back). So the information is there; the score just doesn't weigh it.
3. **The number check works:** 100% of changed numbers caught, at 60 ("check the details"), in every language.
4. **About half of the false alarms come from the number check, too:** 19 of 37 false alarms were digits written as words on the way back ("2 goals" ↩ "two goals", "800 miles" ↩ "Eight hundred miles"). Reading number words as numbers would remove them.
5. **Most of the other false alarms are loose but correct translations** (the professional translator reworded the sentence, e.g. "an Avenger class mine countermeasures ship" ↩ "the Avenger-class minesweeper"), and transliterated names coming back differently ("Hunmin Jeongeum" ↩ "Huan Dan Chinh Am"). Japanese and Korean have the most (10% and 6%).
6. **Google's own translations score higher than professional ones** (96% vs 90% kept). Partly that's real (Google's back-translation reverses its own wording), and partly it's the blind spot: when one engine translates both ways, it can agree with itself ([#29](https://github.com/chrisqtruong/vox2/issues/29)).
7. **Languages differ less than error types do.** Error detection ranges from 22% (Spanish, Tagalog, French, Hindi) to 40% (Korean); the gap between error types (5% to 100%) is far bigger.

## Grades

Letter grades on each measure: A ≥ 90%, B 80–89%, C 70–79%, D 60–69%, F below 60%. For the "lower is better" measures, the grade is on the complement (e.g. 4% false alarms → 96% → A).

| Measure | Result | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Good translations shown as "meaning kept" | 93% | **A** |
| Good translations not falsely flagged "likely off" | 96% | **A** |
| Changed numbers caught | 100% | **A** |
| Changed pronouns caught | 18% | **F** |
| Dropped clauses caught | 15% | **F** |
| Flipped negations caught | 8% | **F** |
| Opposite words caught | 5% | **F** |
| **All meaning errors caught** | **29%** | **F** |

**Overall:** trustworthy when it says "meaning kept" for a correct translation, not yet trustworthy as a guard against translations that say the opposite. Don't market it as "know it came out right" until error detection improves.

## What to improve next (ranked by expected effect)

1. **Targeted checks** ([#28](https://github.com/chrisqtruong/vox2/issues/28)): compare negation words, he/she/his/her, opposite pairs, names and dates between your text and the ↩ line; cap the score and say what changed. On this data, the negation rule alone catches 92% of negation errors, at a cost of 1.8% false alarms on good translations. Also read number words as numbers (removes about half of the false alarms).
2. **Show what changed** ([#27](https://github.com/chrisqtruong/vox2/issues/27)): highlight differing words, so a missed error is still easy to spot.
3. **Translate back with a different engine** ([#29](https://github.com/chrisqtruong/vox2/issues/29)), and the **optional AI check** ([#7](https://github.com/chrisqtruong/vox2/issues/7)).
4. **Per-language thresholds** only if they're still needed after 1–3; languages differed less than error types here.

## Limits of this test

- **40 sentences per language** (8 per error type). Percentages are directional: a single sentence moves a per-language, per-error number by about 12 points. Totals across all languages (88 sentences per error type) are steadier.
- **Planted errors are simple and single.** Real mistranslations can be subtler (a wrong word sense, the wrong tone) or several at once. Tone and register are not tested.
- **Google is used for every translation** in the test, including the "bad" ones. Translations by Claude, ChatGPT or Gemini, and back-translation by a different engine, are not tested yet.
- **The source language is always English.** Translating *from* Vietnamese, Japanese and so on is not tested.
- FLORES sentences are news, travel and science prose, not chat messages.
